Why not 180 or 220 minus age

Age formulas give you the average for many people, not your number.

1
220 minus age was put together roughly, from a summary of old data. Nobody checked it as an exact tool for one person. For one runner it's often off by about 10 beats, and for some by 20 or more. After 40 it tends to guess too low.
2
Newer age formulas are better on average across many people, but for one person they miss by almost as much.
3
180 minus age (the MAF number) is a rule of thumb for an easy-run ceiling, not a Zone 2 measured in a lab. No study has checked it against a measured first threshold.

Even an exact max heart rate doesn't fix your zones. One runner's threshold sits at about 85% of max, another's at about 95%. Zones built on a percent of max put both of them in the same zone, but the effort is different.

That's why it's better to build zones on your threshold heart rate.

What threshold heart rate is

Your threshold is the hardest effort you can hold steady for a long time, about what you could hold in a one-hour race. Go a bit faster and fatigue builds quickly.

Your threshold heart rate (LTHR, lactate threshold heart rate) is your personal reference point. All the zones below are a percent of it, so they fit where your threshold really is.

The test below gives you a field estimate of it. It's not a lab measurement, but it's good enough to train by zones, and a repeat test shows you how you change.

Who should not do the test

This test is 30 minutes of near race effort. It's hard.

see a doctor first and get the OK
You have heart or blood vessel disease, diabetes or kidney disease.
You get pain or discomfort in your chest, neck, jaw or arm, shortness of breath at rest or with light effort, dizziness, fainting or unusual tiredness.

If any of this shows up during the test, stop right away and see a doctor.

wait with the test
You don't train regularly yet, or you're in your first months of running. Start with easy and moderate runs and go by feel: on an easy run you can talk in full sentences. The test makes sense once you run regularly and can hold an even pace.
You're sick or just getting over an illness.
It's hot outside.

How to prepare

  • rest for 48 hoursNo hard or intense sessions for two days before the test. Easy running is fine.
  • sleep and foodSleep well and eat the way you usually do before a run. Don't come in hungry or low on carbs.
  • waterDrink as usual so you're not thirsty when you start. Dehydration pushes your heart rate up.
  • caffeine, same as alwaysIf you usually have coffee before runs, have the same amount before the test. If you don't, skip it. Caffeine doesn't really change heart rate at steady effort, but it can make you faster in an all-out test, and then your tests aren't comparable.
  • chest strapA chest strap reads almost the same as an ECG. A wrist sensor gets less accurate as the effort goes up. No strap? Use your watch, but always the same one.
  • shoes and kitThe same as in your next tests.

Same conditions every retest

You'll do this test more than once. To compare the numbers, keep things the same.

  • routeFlat. A track is best, or a flat road with no traffic lights or crowds.
  • weatherSimilar temperature and humidity. Heat raises your heart rate at the same pace. Cold keeps it the same or a bit lower.
  • time of dayThe same.
  • deviceThe same strap and the same watch.
  • warm-upThe same every time.
  • alone, not in a raceNo training partners and not at a race. With other people you run differently, and the test stops being comparable.

The test, step by step

1
Warm up for 10 to 15 minutes. Easy running, the same every time.
2
Start the test. Press start and run 30 minutes on your own, as if it were a race: as hard as you can, but even for all 30 minutes.
3
Don't start too fast. The most common mistake is going out hard and fading at the end. The first minutes should feel a little easier than you want.
4
At 10 minutes, press lap. Don't stop, just press the button.
5
Run to 30:00 and stop the recording. Then jog easy to cool down.
6
Threshold heart rate = your average heart rate for the last 20 minutes (the lap from minute 10 to minute 30).
7
Threshold pace = your average pace for all 30 minutes (not the last 20. People mix this up a lot).

How to use your zones

1
Do most of your running in Z1 and Z2. Easy runs, long runs, recovery runs. If your heart rate drifts into Z3 on an easy run, slow down, even if the pace feels silly.
2
Work above Z2 (tempo, threshold, intervals) follows your plan. It's not for every run.
3
Your threshold pace from the test is useful too. Pace zones are built from it, and pace is easier to follow when heart rate is off (heat, altitude).

When to retest

1
At the start of each new training phase: when you start your base period and when you start the build toward a race.
2
It's worth checking your threshold pace about every 4 to 6 weeks. The more regularly you test, the more accurate your zones get.
!
My advice as a coach: after an illness or a long break, don't use your old zones. Train normally for 2 to 3 weeks, then test again.

What moves your HR: heat, water, altitude

Your zones are set in normal conditions. In other conditions the same pace gives a different heart rate.

Cool Heart rate stays flat
Hot Higher, and creeps up during the run
Cold The same or a bit lower
Altitude Higher in the first days, normal by week 3

On hot days run by zones and accept a slower pace.

+3-5
bpm for each 1% of body weight you lose
A long run in the heat without water can push your heart rate up a lot.
  • heatBlood goes to your skin to cool you down, so your heart beats faster at the same pace. And it keeps creeping up during the run.
  • humiditySweat dries worse, your body cools worse, and you run out of energy sooner. There's no reliable «plus this many beats» number, but in humid heat keep the effort lower.
  • coldAt the same pace your heart rate is usually the same or a bit lower, and your max can be lower too. Warm clothes, snow and wind can push it up.
  • altitudeIn the first days your heart rate at the same pace is a few beats higher, and your max heart rate is lower. After 2 to 3 weeks your body adapts and your heart rate at that pace comes back to normal.
