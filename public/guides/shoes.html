How to read it

Stack: sole thickness under the heel and under the forefoot, in mm (for example, 38/30).
Drop: the difference between those two numbers, in mm.
Weight: men's US 9, brand figure. I give the lab weight in brackets when it's clearly different.
Budget: "budget" means Kiprun, Chinese brands and last year's versions. "Mid" is the main line of the big brands. "Top" is flagships and racing shoes.
Brand and lab numbers often differ by 1-4 mm and 10-30 g. That's normal. Look at the ballpark.
Tags: "1 source" means only one source confirmed the specs. "Sources differ" means sources give different numbers.

section 07

Carbon: what the science says

Super shoes save energy, but the gain is different for everyone, and the risks are poorly studied.

Running economy

The first lab study on the Vaporfly prototype: about 4% energy savings on average compared with a regular racing shoe.
Seven carbon models at 16 km/h: Alphafly, Vaporfly and Metaspeed Sky cut oxygen use by 2.5-3%. Two models were no different from regular racing shoes.
A 2025 review of 17 studies (281 runners): a moderate gain in economy and in time trials.

And for slower runners?

At 12 km/h the Vaporfly Next% 2 gave about 1.4%, at 10 km/h about 0.9%, and that could be chance.
Recreational runners at 9.4 and 11.5 km/h got 3.9-5%. But that study compared the Endorphin Speed 2 with a budget shoe, so part of the gain came from the foam and the weight.
At 7.5-12 km/h about 2.5%, with no link to speed. The sample was small, and the lab was partly funded by a manufacturer.
In that review, the gain didn't clearly depend on how fast people ran.

Risks

Five cases of navicular bone stress injuries in runners wearing carbon shoes. This doesn't prove the shoes caused them. The authors advise switching gradually.
Elite runners in carbon shoes had a lower cadence and more arch collapse. These changes are linked to stress injuries. The study measured biomechanics, not injury rates.
A survey of recreational runners: 13% reported an injury after switching to carbon, but there was no control group.

Wear

After 450 km a PEBA shoe lost about 2% in running economy and ended up level with the EVA version, which didn't change. Race foams wear out faster than training foams.
Bottom line. Recreational runners get a small gain too, but it's smaller and varies a lot from person to person. Some runners get almost nothing.

Who needs carbon, and who doesn't

Go for it if

you run consistently 3 times a week or more
you're training for a race and going for a time
your distances are the half marathon and marathon
you use carbon for races and 1-2 key workouts a week, switch gradually and rotate it with a regular pair

Not yet if

you're just starting to run
you've had stress fractures in your foot
your ankle is unstable
you run for health. You're better off with a good daily pair and a tempo shoe without a plate (Evo SL, Superblast).
section 08

Practical rules

What we know about size, replacing shoes, rotation, pronation and drop, and how much you can trust it.

Size

Leave about half to a full thumb's width between your longest toe and the end of the shoe. This is expert advice, not research: one well-known review site openly calls it "not research-based".

Measure in the evening. Your feet swell by evening and during a run. For a marathon and for downhills you need extra length. Foot size changes over the years, so check it again.

When to replace

The usual 500-800 km (300-500 miles) is a convention, not a proven limit. There's no research linking a specific mileage to injuries.

What to look for: the foam feels dead, a new pain shows up, the outsole is worn through to the foam, the shoe is warped. Race foams don't last as long.

Rotation

Runners who ran in several pairs had a lower injury risk: HR 0.61, about 39% lower (a study of 264 runners). It's an observational study, so cause and effect aren't proven. The variety of load probably helps.

Comfort filter

Pick the pair that feels the most comfortable when you try it on and on a run. It's a sensible strategy, though the link to injuries here is only assumed.

Pronation

In 927 beginners running in neutral shoes, moderate pronation didn't raise injury risk.

In the US Army, choosing shoes by foot shape didn't reduce injuries (more than 9,000 soldiers). Choose a stability shoe by feel, not by a "diagnosis" in the store.

Drop

In a randomized study (553 runners, drops of 0, 6 and 10 mm), drop overall didn't affect injury risk. For occasional runners a low drop lowered the risk, for regular runners it raised it.

The takeaway: don't change drop sharply.

section 09

Glossary in plain words

StackSole thickness under the heel and forefoot. Higher means softer and more protective, but less stable. In road races World Athletics limits stack to 40 mm.
DropThe height difference between heel and forefoot. 8-10 mm is classic, 4-6 is medium, 0 is "zero drop".
RockerA sole curved like a rocking chair that rolls your foot forward.
PlateA stiff insert in the sole. Nylon or plastic plates are more flexible, carbon is stiffer and pushes harder. It only works together with springy foam.
EVAClassic foam: cheap and durable, but flat.
TPU, eTPU, A-TPUSpringy foams: durable, stable, lively (PWRRUN+, FF Leap, Nitro Elite).
PEBA (Pebax)The lightest and springiest foam, the base of super shoes (ZoomX, Lightstrike Pro, PWRRUN PB). Wears out faster.
Supercritical foamFoamed with gas under pressure (nitrogen, CO₂). Lighter and livelier than regular foam.
UpperEngineered mesh breathes, knit fits like a sock, jacquard is dense and warm. A gusseted tongue is sewn to the sides and holds the foot better.
GTX (Gore-Tex)A waterproof membrane: dry feet in slush, but hot and 20-40 g heavier.
LugsThe studs on the outsole. 3-4 mm for hard trails, 5 mm and more for mud and snow. Vibram Megagrip is seen as the benchmark for grip.
Toe boxThe front part of the shoe. A wide one gives your toes room, a narrow one holds them tighter.
Stability techA wide platform, high sidewalls ("rails" like GuideRails or H-Frame), firmer foam on the inner side. They don't "fix" your foot. They gently limit how much it rolls in.
section 10

Quick pick, step by step

1
Where do you run? Road: road shoes. Dirt, forest, mountains: trail shoes. Slush and snow: GTX versions.
2
How many pairs do you have? One: a neutral daily trainer (section 1). Two or more: add a soft pair for recovery (section 2) or a tempo shoe (section 4).
3
Check your stability. If in a neutral pair your foot or knee starts to collapse inward by the end of a long run, try a stability shoe (section 3). Decide by feel, not by a pronation test.
4
Try them on in the evening, in running socks. Leave half a toe to a full toe of room, and jog around the store or on a treadmill. Take the pair that feels best.
5
Training for a race and going for a time? If you run more than 3 times a week regularly: carbon (section 5), but first 2-4 weeks of short efforts in it. Otherwise: tempo shoes without a plate.
6
Save smart. Last year's version of a model almost always gives you 90% of the benefit. Kiprun and Chinese brands (Xtep, Li-Ning, Anta) give you top-level foams for less money.
7
Replace by feel. Look at how they feel and how worn the outsole is, not at the mileage. Rotate your pairs.
